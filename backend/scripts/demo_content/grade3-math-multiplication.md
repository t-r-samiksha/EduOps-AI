# Grade 3 Mathematics — Multiplication and Regrouping

Riverside Public School · Term 1, 2026–27 · Class notes prepared for Grade 3 A and Grade 3 B

---

## 1. What multiplication really means

Multiplication is repeated addition. When we write `4 × 6`, we are saying "four groups
of six". You could add `6 + 6 + 6 + 6` and get the same answer, 24. Multiplication is
just a faster way of writing that down.

At Riverside we always read the multiplication sign as **"groups of"**, never as
"times". So `7 × 3` is read aloud as "seven groups of three". This matters when you
draw the picture: seven circles with three dots inside each circle. If you read it the
other way round you will draw three circles with seven dots, and although the answer is
the same number, your picture will not match the word problem.

### The commutative rule

`4 × 6` and `6 × 4` both equal 24. Swapping the two numbers never changes the answer.
We call this the **commutative rule**, and it is the single most useful shortcut in
Grade 3, because it halves the number of table facts you have to memorise. If you know
`8 × 3 = 24`, you already know `3 × 8 = 24` for free.

## 2. The Riverside Ladder Method

When you multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number, we use what this school
calls the **Ladder Method**. It is not the only way to multiply, and you may have seen
a different layout at another school or from an older sibling. In Grade 3 at Riverside,
please use the Ladder, because the marks in your Term 1 test are given for the steps,
not only for the final answer.

The Ladder has exactly three rungs:

1. **Split** the two-digit number into tens and ones.
2. **Multiply** each part separately.
3. **Add** the two results together.

### Worked example: 34 × 6

**Rung 1 — Split.** 34 becomes 30 and 4.

**Rung 2 — Multiply each part.**
- 30 × 6 = 180
- 4 × 6 = 24

**Rung 3 — Add.**
- 180 + 24 = **204**

So `34 × 6 = 204`.

Write all three rungs on separate lines in your notebook. A correct answer with no
ladder shown earns 1 mark out of 3. A wrong final answer with all three rungs shown
correctly earns 2 marks out of 3. This is deliberate: we are marking your method.

### Worked example: 57 × 8

**Rung 1 — Split.** 57 becomes 50 and 7.

**Rung 2 — Multiply.**
- 50 × 8 = 400
- 7 × 8 = 56

**Rung 3 — Add.**
- 400 + 56 = **456**

So `57 × 8 = 456`.

### Worked example: 26 × 4

**Rung 1 — Split.** 26 becomes 20 and 6.

**Rung 2 — Multiply.**
- 20 × 4 = 80
- 6 × 4 = 24

**Rung 3 — Add.**
- 80 + 24 = **104**

So `26 × 4 = 104`.

## 3. Regrouping — what "carrying the one" actually means

Many children say "carry the one" without knowing what is being carried or where it
goes. Here is the truth: **you are not carrying a one. You are carrying a ten.**

Look at `26 × 4` again, but this time in the column layout:

```
   2 6
 ×   4
 -----
```

Start at the ones column. `6 × 4 = 24`. But 24 will not fit in the ones column, because
the ones column can only hold a single digit from 0 to 9. So we split 24 into **2 tens
and 4 ones**. The 4 stays in the ones column. The 2 tens move over to the tens column.

That small number you write above the tens column is **2, and it means 2 tens, which is
20**. It is not the number 2. This is why we call it **regrouping** at Riverside and not
"carrying" — nothing is being carried anywhere, the number is being regrouped from one
place value into another.

Now the tens column: `2 × 4 = 8`, plus the 2 we regrouped = 10. Write 10. The answer is
104, which matches the Ladder answer above. Both methods must always agree. If they do
not, you have made an arithmetic slip somewhere — go back and check the ones column
first, because that is where the error is about 8 times out of 10.

### When do you regroup?

You regroup whenever a column's result is **10 or more**. If the column result is 9 or
less, there is nothing to regroup and you write it straight down.

A common mistake: regrouping when the answer is exactly 10. Some children think 10 is
"still a single number" and write it in one column. It is not. 10 is one ten and zero
ones. Write 0 in that column and regroup the 1.

## 4. Multiplying by 10, 100 and 1000

To multiply a whole number by 10, put one zero at the end. `43 × 10 = 430`.

To multiply by 100, put two zeros. `43 × 100 = 4300`.

To multiply by 1000, put three zeros. `43 × 1000 = 43000`.

**Careful:** we say "put a zero at the end" because in Grade 3 you are only multiplying
whole numbers. This rule is a shortcut that works for whole numbers only, and you will
learn in Grade 5 why it stops working once decimals appear. For now, whole numbers only.

## 5. The times tables you must know by heart

By the end of Term 1 you are expected to know these from memory, with no counting on
fingers:

- The 2 times table, up to 2 × 12
- The 5 times table, up to 5 × 12
- The 10 times table, up to 10 × 12
- The 3 times table, up to 3 × 12

The 4, 6, 7, 8 and 9 tables come in Term 2. You are not expected to know them yet, but
you may use the commutative rule to work them out: if a question asks for `7 × 5` and
you have only learned the 5 table, swap it around and think of it as `5 × 7 = 35`.

### The 9 trick (optional, but useful)

For the 9 times table, hold up ten fingers. To find `9 × 4`, fold down your fourth
finger. You now have 3 fingers to the left of the folded one and 6 to the right. The
answer is 36. This works for every 9 fact from `9 × 1` to `9 × 10`.

## 6. Word problems

The hardest part of a word problem is not the arithmetic — it is deciding whether to
multiply at all. Look for these signals:

- "each" — *"Each box holds 8 pencils. There are 6 boxes."* → multiply
- "every" — *"Every child gets 3 stickers. There are 24 children."* → multiply
- "in total" combined with equal groups → multiply
- "altogether" combined with equal groups → multiply

If the groups are **not** equal, you cannot multiply. *"Riya has 4 sweets, Arjun has 7
sweets, and Meera has 2 sweets"* is an addition problem, not a multiplication problem,
because the groups are different sizes.

### Worked word problem

*The school library has 23 shelves. Each shelf holds 7 books. How many books are there
altogether?*

The word "each" tells us the groups are equal. 23 groups of 7.

- Split: 23 → 20 and 3
- Multiply: 20 × 7 = 140, and 3 × 7 = 21
- Add: 140 + 21 = **161 books**

Always write the unit in your answer. "161" on its own loses half a mark. "161 books"
is a complete answer.

## 7. Checking your answer with estimation

Before you write your final answer, estimate. Round the two-digit number to the nearest
ten and multiply in your head.

For `57 × 8`: round 57 to 60. `60 × 8 = 480`. Our answer was 456, which is close to 480,
so it is believable.

If you had accidentally written 4560 or 45, the estimate would have told you instantly
that something was wrong. Estimating takes five seconds and catches most careless
errors.
