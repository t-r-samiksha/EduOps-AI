# Grade 3 English — Nouns, Adjectives and Building a Paragraph

Riverside Public School · Term 1, 2026–27 · Class notes for Grade 3 A and Grade 3 B

---

## 1. Nouns — naming words

A **noun** is a naming word. It names a person, a place, a thing, or an animal.

- Person: *teacher, Meera, doctor, brother*
- Place: *school, Chennai, kitchen, playground*
- Thing: *pencil, table, kite, sandwich*
- Animal: *elephant, sparrow, goat, ant*

### Common nouns and proper nouns

A **common noun** is a general name for any one of a kind: *city, girl, river, month*.

A **proper noun** is the particular name of one specific person, place or thing:
*Chennai, Meera, Ganga, August*.

**Proper nouns always start with a capital letter, wherever they appear in the
sentence.** This is the rule Grade 3 loses the most marks on, so read this carefully:

> The **c**ity of **C**hennai is hot in **A**ugust.

"city" is common and stays lowercase. "Chennai" and "August" are proper and take
capitals even though they sit in the middle of the sentence.

**Days and months are proper nouns.** *Monday, Friday, January, August* all take capital
letters. This catches people out because they do not feel like names in the way that
*Meera* does — but in English they are, and they are marked as such.

**Seasons are not proper nouns.** *summer, winter, monsoon* stay lowercase. So:

> We go to Ooty every **s**ummer, usually in **M**ay.

"summer" is lowercase, "May" is capitalised. Both are in the same sentence, which is why
this is a favourite test question.

### Singular and plural

Most nouns become plural by adding **-s**: *book → books, chair → chairs*.

Nouns ending in **-s, -sh, -ch, -x** add **-es**: *bus → buses, brush → brushes,
bench → benches, box → boxes*.

Nouns ending in a consonant + **-y** change the y to **-ies**: *baby → babies,
city → cities, story → stories*. But if a vowel comes before the y, just add -s:
*boy → boys, key → keys, day → days*.

Some nouns change completely and must simply be learned:

| Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- |
| child | children |
| man | men |
| woman | women |
| foot | feet |
| tooth | teeth |
| mouse | mice |

And a few do not change at all: *one sheep, two sheep. One deer, two deer.*

## 2. Adjectives — describing words

An **adjective** describes a noun. It tells us more about it.

> The **tall** boy carried a **heavy** bag.

*tall* describes *boy*. *heavy* describes *bag*.

Adjectives can describe:

- **Size** — *tiny, huge, tall, narrow*
- **Colour** — *scarlet, golden, pale*
- **Shape** — *round, square, crooked*
- **Number** — *seven, many, few*
- **Feeling or quality** — *gentle, noisy, brave, stubborn*

### The Riverside rule: no boring adjectives

In this class, four adjectives are on the **banned list** for creative writing:

**nice, good, bad, very**

They are banned because they tell the reader almost nothing. "The food was nice" does
not put a picture in anyone's head. Replace them with something specific:

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| a nice day | a bright, breezy day |
| good food | steaming, buttery food |
| a bad smell | a sour, rotting smell |
| very big | enormous, towering |

Note that **very** is banned as a repair tool. "very big" is weaker than "enormous",
because one strong adjective always beats a weak one propped up by "very". If you find
yourself writing "very", the real fix is to find a better adjective.

Using a banned word in your Term 1 writing piece costs half a mark each, up to a maximum
of two marks. Using them in casual speech is fine — this rule is for written work.

## 3. Building a paragraph — the Riverside Burger

A paragraph is a group of sentences about **one** idea. At Riverside we build one using
the **Burger** shape, which has three layers:

**The top bun — the topic sentence.** One sentence saying what the whole paragraph is
about.

**The filling — three detail sentences.** Three sentences that each add something new
about that idea.

**The bottom bun — the closing sentence.** One sentence that rounds the idea off. It
must not simply repeat the top bun in different words.

So a Grade 3 paragraph is **five sentences**: one + three + one. Not four, not eight.
Five. In the Term 1 writing task, a paragraph with the wrong number of sentences loses
one mark for structure even if the content is lovely.

### Worked example

> **My grandmother's kitchen is the warmest room in her house.** The old stove hisses
> from early morning until night. Copper pots hang in a crooked row above the window,
> catching the light. The whole room smells of cardamom and hot ghee. **No matter how
> cold the morning is outside, nobody in that house ever wants to leave it.**

The top bun names the idea: the kitchen is warm. The three filling sentences each add
something different — sound, sight, smell. The bottom bun closes it off with a new
thought rather than repeating "it is the warmest room".

### A weak example, and why

> My grandmother's kitchen is very nice. It is a nice room. There are pots. My
> grandmother's kitchen is very nice.

This fails on four counts. It uses banned adjectives (*very, nice*). The filling adds
almost no detail. It is four sentences, not five. And the closing sentence just repeats
the opening word for word.

## 4. Capital letters and full stops

Every sentence begins with a **capital letter** and ends with one of:

- a **full stop** ( . ) for a statement
- a **question mark** ( ? ) for a question
- an **exclamation mark** ( ! ) for a shout or a strong feeling

The word **I** is always a capital letter, wherever it appears:

> Meera and **I** went to the market.

### The comma in a list

When you list three or more things, separate them with commas and put **and** before the
last item:

> I packed a book, a water bottle, a towel and my slippers.

Riverside uses **no comma before the "and"**. You may see a comma there in American books
— that is a different house style and it is not wrong in itself, but in your Term 1
writing please follow the Riverside style and leave it out.

## 5. The Term 1 reader — *The Sparrow and the Clay Pot*

Our class reader this term is the short story **"The Sparrow and the Clay Pot"**, which
we read together in Week 3.

**The story:** A thirsty sparrow finds a tall clay pot with a little water at the very
bottom. Her beak cannot reach it. She tries tipping the pot and it will not move. She
tries calling other birds and none come. At last she picks up small pebbles one by one
and drops them into the pot. The water rises a little with each pebble, until it is high
enough for her to drink.

**Characters:** the sparrow (the only named character in our version is **Chirika**), and
the clay pot, which is not a character but is important enough that the story is named
after it.

**The moral we agreed in class discussion:** *Patience solves what panic cannot.*

You may have heard this story before with a crow instead of a sparrow, and the moral
given as "necessity is the mother of invention". Our version has a sparrow named Chirika,
and the moral we wrote on the board and will use in the test is the patience one. If a
test question asks for the moral of the story, write the version we agreed in class.

**Three questions we answered on it:**

1. Why did the sparrow not simply fly away and find another water source? *Because she
   had already flown a long distance and was too tired to go further.*
2. What did she try before the pebbles? *She tried tipping the pot over, and she tried
   calling other birds for help.*
3. Why did the water rise? *Because the pebbles took up space at the bottom and pushed
   the water upwards.*

## 6. Term 1 assessment note

The Term 1 English paper is **25 marks** across three sections:

- **Grammar, 10 marks** — identifying nouns and adjectives, common versus proper nouns,
  singular to plural, capital letters
- **Reading comprehension, 8 marks** — a passage from *The Sparrow and the Clay Pot*
  with four questions
- **Writing, 7 marks** — one Burger paragraph on a given topic. Marks: 3 for content,
  1 for the five-sentence structure, 1 for capital letters and full stops, 2 for
  adjective choice (with up to 2 deducted for banned words)

Write in pencil. Leave one full line between your rough plan and your final paragraph.
