# Grade 3 Science — How Plants Make Their Food

Riverside Public School · Term 1, 2026–27 · Class notes for Grade 3 A and Grade 3 B

---

## 1. Plants are the only living things that make their own food

Animals must eat. A cow eats grass, a tiger eats a deer, and you eat roti and dal. None
of them can make food inside their own body. Plants are different. A plant takes simple
things from the air, the soil and the sunlight, and builds its own food out of them.

The name for this process is **photosynthesis**. Break the word in half and it explains
itself:

- **photo** means *light*
- **synthesis** means *putting together*

So photosynthesis means "putting together with light". Say it as **foh-toh-SIN-thuh-sis**.

---

## 2. The four things a plant needs

A plant needs exactly four things to make food:

1. **Sunlight** — the energy source
2. **Carbon dioxide** — a gas taken in from the air through the leaves
3. **Water** — pulled up from the soil by the roots
4. **Chlorophyll** — the green substance already inside the leaf

Notice that **soil is not on this list.** This surprises most people. Soil holds the
plant upright and holds the water the roots drink, but the plant does not eat soil. You
can grow a plant with its roots in plain water and no soil at all, and it will still
make food perfectly well. We proved this in class — see the experiment in section 5.

### What the plant makes

Out of those four ingredients the plant produces two things:

- **Glucose** — a sugar, which is the plant's food
- **Oxygen** — a gas, which the plant releases back into the air

That released oxygen is the air you are breathing right now. This is the single most
important fact in this chapter: **plants make the oxygen that animals need, and animals
breathe out the carbon dioxide that plants need.** Each one produces what the other
needs. That exchange is why a world with no plants would very quickly become a world
with no animals.

## 3. The parts of a plant and what each one does

| Part | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Roots | Hold the plant in the soil; drink water and minerals |
| Stem | Carries water up to the leaves; holds the plant upright |
| Leaves | Make the food — this is where photosynthesis happens |
| Flowers | Make seeds so new plants can grow |
| Fruit | Protects the seeds and helps them travel |

### Why leaves are flat and wide

A leaf is flat and wide so it can catch as much sunlight as possible, in the same way
that a wide umbrella catches more rain than a narrow one. A leaf that was thin and
round like a pencil would catch far less light and the plant would grow slowly.

### Stomata — the tiny doors

The underside of a leaf is covered in holes so small you cannot see them without a
microscope. Each hole is called a **stoma** (say **STOH-muh**). More than one are called
**stomata** (say **STOH-muh-tuh**).

Carbon dioxide goes in through the stomata. Oxygen and extra water vapour come out
through them. The stomata can open and close: they open in the daytime when the plant
is making food, and most of them close at night when it is not.

**They are on the underside of the leaf, not the top.** This is a good design. If they
were on top, rain and dust would block them, and the strong midday sun would dry the
leaf out through the open holes.

## 4. Chlorophyll and why leaves are green

**Chlorophyll** (say **KLOR-uh-fil**) is the green colouring inside a leaf. It is not
there to make the plant pretty. Its job is to catch sunlight energy, the way a solar
panel on a roof catches sunlight.

Here is the part most people get backwards. Chlorophyll looks green because green is
the **one colour it does not use**. It absorbs red light and blue light and puts that
energy to work. Green light is reflected back out and bounces into your eyes. So a leaf
is green because green is the light the plant is *rejecting*, not the light it is
keeping.

A leaf with no chlorophyll — the white parts of a variegated money plant, for instance —
cannot make food at all. Those white patches survive only on food made by the green
parts of the same plant.

## 5. Our classroom experiment — the four bean plants

In the second week of Term 1 we planted four bean seeds in four pots. We labelled them
Pot A, Pot B, Pot C and Pot D, put them in different conditions, and measured them every
Monday for four weeks. Both Grade 3 A and Grade 3 B ran the same experiment.

**The conditions:**

- **Pot A** — sunlight on the windowsill, watered 100 ml every second day *(this is our
  control — everything normal)*
- **Pot B** — inside the dark stationery cupboard, watered 100 ml every second day
- **Pot C** — sunlight on the windowsill, no water at all
- **Pot D** — sunlight on the windowsill, watered 100 ml every second day, but with all
  its leaves smeared with petroleum jelly on the **underside**

**Height in centimetres, measured each Monday:**

| Pot | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Leaf colour at Week 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 3 cm | 7 cm | 12 cm | 16 cm | deep green |
| B | 3 cm | 8 cm | 11 cm | 11 cm | pale yellow |
| C | 3 cm | 4 cm | 4 cm | 4 cm | brown, dry |
| D | 3 cm | 5 cm | 6 cm | 6 cm | green but drooping |

**What we concluded:**

**Pot A** grew best. It had all four things it needed.

**Pot B** is the interesting one, and the result confused several children. In Week 2 it
was actually *taller* than Pot A — 8 cm against 7 cm. That is not a mistake in our
measuring. A plant kept in the dark grows tall and stretched, in a desperate attempt to
reach light it can sense is somewhere above it. But it is stretching, not thriving: it
was using up the food stored inside the seed, not making any of its own. By Week 3 the
stored food ran out, growth stopped dead at 11 cm, and the leaves turned pale yellow
because the plant could not keep making chlorophyll. **A taller plant is not always a
healthier plant.**

**Pot C** stopped almost immediately. Without water there is no photosynthesis at all,
and the plant died by Week 2.

**Pot D** is the one that proves the stomata point. It had sunlight. It had water. It
had chlorophyll — the leaves stayed green all the way to Week 4. The only thing it did
not have was a way to take in carbon dioxide, because the petroleum jelly had sealed the
stomata on the underside of every leaf. It stalled at 6 cm. This shows that a plant can
have three of the four ingredients and still fail. All four are needed, not most of them.

## 6. Word equation for photosynthesis

You must be able to write this out in the Term 1 test:

> **carbon dioxide + water --(sunlight and chlorophyll)--> glucose + oxygen**

Read it as: carbon dioxide and water, in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll,
produce glucose and oxygen.

In Grade 3 the word equation is all that is required. The chemical formula version comes
in Grade 6 — do not worry about it now.

---

## 7. When does a plant do this?

Photosynthesis happens **only in daylight**, because sunlight is one of the four
ingredients. At night it stops completely.

But here is something children often get wrong: **plants breathe all the time, day and
night.** Breathing (respiration) and food-making (photosynthesis) are two different
processes. During the day a healthy plant makes far more oxygen than it uses up
breathing, so overall it gives oxygen out. At night it makes none, so overall it takes a
small amount of oxygen in.

The old advice about not sleeping under a tree at night comes from this fact. The amount
of oxygen a tree uses at night is very small and completely harmless, so the advice is
not really something to worry about — but the science underneath it is real.

## 8. Words to learn for the Term 1 test

- **photosynthesis** — the process of making food using light
- **chlorophyll** — the green substance that catches sunlight
- **stomata** — tiny holes on the underside of a leaf
- **glucose** — the sugar a plant makes as its food
- **carbon dioxide** — the gas a plant takes in
- **oxygen** — the gas a plant gives out
- **control** — the pot in an experiment kept in normal conditions, so you have
  something to compare the others against

The Term 1 Science test is **15 marks**. Five marks are for the word equation and the
four ingredients. Five marks are for labelling a diagram of a leaf, including the
stomata on the correct side. Five marks are for explaining one of the four pots from
our experiment and saying what it proved.
